### Session 1: The Problem-Solving Mindset: Choosing AI vs. Automation

#### 1.1 The problem-solving mindset: AI vs. non-AI automation

**The mental model.** Before anyone opens AI Studio, the highest-leverage question is not "how do I prompt this well" — it's "does this task even need AI?" Two failure modes cost real time and money, and they pull in opposite directions: **under-using automation** (a person manually doing what a five-line rule could do instantly, every day, forever) and **over-using AI** (routing a task to a large language model that a deterministic script would solve faster, cheaper, and with guaranteed reproducibility). Both are common, and both are avoidable with one decision framework applied *before* reaching for a tool.

The framework rests on two axes:

1. **Is the task well-defined and repeatable**, or does it require judgment that shifts case to case?
2. **Is the input/output structured** (fits a fixed schema — numbers, fields, known categories), or **unstructured** (natural language, free-form, requires reading and synthesis)?

| | Structured input/output | Unstructured input/output |
|---|---|---|
| **Well-defined, repeatable** | Deterministic rule/script — e.g., flag any trade above a fixed notional threshold | Classical NLP/ML classification — e.g., sort inbound client emails into five known categories |
| **Judgment-dependent, ambiguous** | Statistical/ML model with human review — e.g., a calibrated risk score that a human still signs off on | Generative AI — e.g., explain *why* a trading pattern looks unusual relative to precedent |

This is not a novel taxonomy invented for this course — it tracks closely with how the robotic-process-automation (RPA) and AI-agent industry itself now frames the boundary. RPA is the right tool "when a process is repetitive and rule-based, the inputs are structured and predictable, and no decision-making is required" — precisely the kind of precision that finance, compliance, and reporting functions need, where even a small deviation creates risk. AI agents earn their keep on the opposite profile: unstructured data, exception handling, and multi-step decisions, where — critically — "you define the outcome, not the steps" ([TMS Outsource, "RPA vs. Agentic AI"](https://tms-outsource.com/blog/posts/rpa-vs-agentic-ai/); [SS&C Blue Prism, "Agentic AI vs RPA"](https://www.blueprism.com/resources/blog/agentic-ai-vs-rpa-vs-ai-agents-comparing/)). The two are not enemies: in practice, an increasing number of real systems are hybrids, where RPA bridges legacy systems and structured back-end updates while an LLM-based layer handles the interpretation and judgment calls upstream of it.

**Why this matters here specifically.** This audience already has both tools within reach — deterministic scripts/rules in their existing systems, and GenAI via AI Studio. The risk isn't a lack of access to AI; it's reaching for it reflexively because it's the fashionable option, when a one-line rule would be faster, free of hallucination risk, and fully auditable — a genuinely important property in a regulated trading environment where every material output may need to be explainable after the fact.

**The practical habit.** Before opening AI Studio for a task, ask: *"could I write this as a rule in one sentence, and would that rule hold up every time?"* If yes, it very likely doesn't need AI. If the honest answer is "it depends" or "you'd have to read it and judge," that is GenAI's actual territory.

**Pros/cons, stated plainly.**
- Deterministic automation: reproducible, fast, cheap per run, fully auditable — but rigid, and it breaks (or silently mis-handles) anything outside its coded cases.
- GenAI: handles ambiguity, scale of unstructured text, and novel phrasing it was never explicitly programmed for — but it is non-deterministic (§1.2 below explains exactly why), can be confidently wrong, costs more per call, and is harder to audit in the traditional sense.

`[DRAFT finance illustration — for structure only, not yet a chosen example]`: "Flag any trade over $10M notional" is a one-line deterministic rule — no ambiguity, no AI needed. "Explain why this trade looks unusual relative to the desk's typical flow" cannot be reduced to a rule, because "unusual" requires synthesizing context, precedent, and judgment — that's GenAI's job.

#### 1.2 From rules to probabilities: the traditional-AI-to-GenAI journey

**The 60-second "what is AI" anchor.** AI, at its broadest, is software performing tasks that would normally require human intelligence — recognizing patterns, predicting outcomes, generating content. Within that umbrella, capability has arrived in roughly this order: **rule-based systems** (if-this-then-that logic, no learning) → **machine learning** (a system learns patterns from historical data — a fraud classifier trained on past labeled transactions) → **deep learning** (neural networks capable of learning far more complex patterns — image recognition, speech, sequence modeling) → **generative AI** (models that don't just classify or predict a fixed label, but produce new content — text, code, images — built on large language models trained on massive text corpora). This is genuinely a 60-second anchor, not a lecture: this room already uses GenAI tools daily, and dwelling on it wastes the one resource this format can't spare — slide-minutes.

**What actually changed, mechanically.** Pre-LLM software, and most classical ML models, return a fixed output for a fixed input. A fraud classifier says "fraud" or "not fraud," with a confidence score, but its decision boundary is fixed once trained and its output space is narrow — bounded to whatever labels it was explicitly trained to predict. Generative AI works differently at a mechanical level: a large language model reads a sequence of tokens and predicts a *probability distribution* over what token comes next, then samples from that distribution, one token at a time — repeating this conditioned on everything generated so far. This is genuinely probabilistic, not just "complex": the same prompt, run twice, can produce two similar but non-identical responses, because the model is sampling rather than looking anything up.

This distinction is well established in current industry framing of the two paradigms: deterministic AI dominates structured, repeatable, high-stakes execution (workflow automation, policy enforcement, fraud detection), while generative AI's underlying mechanism means it deals in likelihoods and patterns rather than certainties, and can produce different outputs even from identical inputs ([Moveo.AI, "Deterministic AI vs. Probabilistic AI"](https://moveo.ai/blog-new/deterministic-ai-vs-probabilistic-ai)). Where the task has no single right answer — drafting, explaining a complex idea in plain language, synthesizing across several documents — this variability is a feature, not a defect; where the task demands one provably correct answer every time, it's the reason GenAI is the wrong tool (back to §1.1's framework).

**Why this matters to this room specifically, and where it's going next in the series.** This single mechanical fact is *why* AI Studio can give a slightly different answer to the same question twice; *why* it can handle a genuinely open-ended ask ("explain this pattern") that a traditional model architecture could never have been coded to do; and *why* its output can never be "proven" the way a deterministic calculation can be proven. Plant this seed here deliberately — it is the direct foundation for Session 4's entire "verify before you trust" theme, and it's also the reason Session 2 exists at all: if output were deterministic, there would be no craft to "prompting" it well.

#### 1.3 GenAI fundamentals and advancements, scoped to what you actually use

The goal here is *not* to survey the frontier AI news cycle — that fails this audience's practicality filter immediately. It's to give three functional concepts that explain behavior this room will actually hit inside AI Studio and AI Lens, plus the one genuine capability shift of the last two years that explains why these platforms work the way they do.

**Context window.** The amount of text — instructions, conversation history, and any attached documents — the model can "see" at once before it has to start dropping or compressing something. This is *why* context engineering (Session 2) is a real discipline and not busywork: attaching too much irrelevant material dilutes the model's attention on what actually matters, while attaching too little starves it of information it needs. This becomes concretely visible the moment someone tries to attach a 150-page policy document and the platform behaves unexpectedly.

**Tokens.** The unit models actually process text in — roughly three to four characters, or about three-quarters of a word, per token in English. Every cost, latency, and context-window limit quoted for a platform is measured in tokens, not words or pages — worth knowing the moment a document attachment behaves oddly or a response gets cut off mid-thought.

**Multi-modality.** Modern foundation models increasingly handle text, images, and structured data together in a single call, not text alone. Current proprietary model families across the major labs — the specific names and versions change fast enough that they're not worth memorizing for this session — have all converged on this as a baseline capability rather than a novelty ([Refonte Learning, "Generative AI Models in 2026"](https://www.refontelearning.com/blog/generative-ai-models-in-2026-top-trends-breakthroughs-and-opportunities)). This maps directly onto this room's own dashboard-generation and document-parsing use cases: a single query can plausibly combine a chart image, a spreadsheet extract, and a natural-language question.

**The one advancement genuinely worth naming: from answering to acting.** The most consequential shift in the last one to two years, and the only "frontier" development this session spends real time on, is that models can now reliably call tools/functions, chain multiple steps, and hand off between specialized agents — rather than only ever producing a single block of text in response to a single question. This is not incidental to this audience: it is the *specific* mechanism behind AI Studio's "select agent(s)/tool(s), then ask" interaction pattern, and it's covered properly as its own topic next (§1.4).

**Why this matters.** Understanding tokens and context windows explains real, recurring behavior ("why did it ignore part of my long message?" → context dilution, not a bug). Understanding the tool-calling shift explains why AI Studio feels categorically different from a plain chat window, and sets up exactly what an "agent" is.

#### 1.4 Meet the agent: what agentic AI actually is

**The mental model.** A plain LLM call is a single round trip: prompt in, response out. An **agent** is an LLM given the ability to reason about what a task requires, decide which tool or data source it needs, call that tool, observe what comes back, and either respond or repeat the cycle — prompt → *(reason → act → observe)* repeated as needed → response.

**Anthropic's own engineering guidance draws a sharper, more useful line than "agent = fancy chatbot,"** and it's worth teaching precisely because it gives this room a real decision tool, not just a definition. Anthropic distinguishes **workflows** — systems where LLMs and tools are orchestrated through *predefined* code paths, i.e., a human decided the steps in advance — from **agents**, where the LLM *dynamically directs its own process and tool use*, deciding the steps itself as it goes ([Anthropic, "Building Effective Agents"](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)). Anthropic's practical guidance is genuinely counter-intuitive for an audience primed to think "more agentic is more advanced, therefore better": start with the simplest thing that works — often a single well-crafted call with good context and a couple of examples is enough — and reach for true agentic behavior specifically when a task is open-ended enough that the number and order of steps *can't* be predicted or hardcoded in advance. Agents trade latency and cost for the ability to handle that unpredictability; that trade is only worth making when the task actually needs it.

**Why this matters here specifically.** This is the literal mechanism behind "ask AI Studio a question, get a dashboard": the underlying agent reasoned that it needed market data, selected a tool to fetch it, observed the result, and rendered a visualization from it — a small reason-act-observe loop, invisible to the person who just typed a question. A "tool" in AI Studio, in this framing, is exactly one of these callable capabilities the agent can reach for; selecting multiple agents/tools for one query means handing the system a bigger toolbox to reason across, which is powerful but not free (more below).

**One agent or several?** Use one focused agent/tool when the task is narrow and clearly matches a specific capability — it's faster and its behavior is easier to predict. Reach for multiple agents/tools when the task genuinely spans domains that no single tool covers — e.g., a query that plausibly needs both a market-data capability and a policy-lookup capability to be answered completely.

**Pros/cons.** Agentic behavior unlocks multi-step, tool-augmented tasks that a single plain prompt structurally cannot do — but every added tool call is an added source of latency and an added point where something can go wrong, and the path from question to answer becomes correspondingly less predictable. This is the direct setup for Session 4's troubleshooting checklist: when something goes wrong, "which step in the chain actually failed?" is the diagnostic question, not "why is the AI broken."

### Session 2: Talking to AI Well: Prompt, Context, and Harness

#### 2.1 Three levers, not one: Prompt, Context, and Harness Engineering

**The mental model.** Most people, when an AI output disappoints them, reach for exactly one lever: rewording the prompt. That instinct is understandable but incomplete, because a large language model's behavior is actually shaped by three genuinely distinct layers, and a failure at any one of them looks identical from the outside — "the AI got it wrong."

- **Prompt engineering** — the words in your immediate instruction: wording, requested format, any examples given.
- **Context engineering** — everything surrounding the prompt that the model can see: attached documents, conversation history, retrieved knowledge, background facts. The current field-wide framing is useful here: prompt engineering asks *"how should I phrase this?"* — a question about one input/output pair — while context engineering asks the bigger question, *"what does the model need to be able to see, right now, to do this well?"* ([Elastic, "Context engineering vs. prompt engineering"](https://www.elastic.co/search-labs/blog/context-engineering-vs-prompt-engineering)). Prompt engineering is a subset of context engineering, not a replacement for it — you still need well-phrased instructions, but for anything beyond a single simple query, the harder and higher-leverage problem is almost always *what information is actually in view*, not *how it's worded*.
- **Harness engineering** — the scaffolding around the model: which tools/agents are available at all, in what order they can be called, what validation wraps the interaction. In AI Studio terms, this is which agent(s)/tool(s) were selected before the question was even typed.

**Why "Harness," not "Loop."** It's worth being explicit about this term, because it's easy to reach for "loop engineering" instead once you've seen the reason-act-observe pattern from Session 1. The loop (ReAct: reason, act, observe, repeat — [Yao et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)) is the *runtime behavior that results* from a well-built harness — it's what happens once the scaffolding exists, not something engineered directly. Harness, prompt, and context are all things you configure *before* a query runs; the loop is the *outcome* of a good harness at runtime. Keeping "Harness" preserves the parallel: three levers you design, one behavior that emerges.

**How the layers relate.** Picture three concentric layers: harness (outermost — what capabilities exist at all for this interaction) → context (middle — what information is actually available) → prompt (innermost — the specific ask). A bad output can originate at *any* layer, not only the innermost one — this is the direct foundation for Session 4's troubleshooting checklist.

**When to reach for which lever.**
- Output is vague, wrong tone, or wrong format → tune the **prompt** (§2.2).
- Output is factually incomplete or ignorant of something you know to be true → tune the **context** — attach the document, add the fact, or use retrieval (Session 3).
- Output can't do the task at all — e.g., it can't fetch live data or take an action → tune the **harness** — select a different agent/tool.

**How to apply.** Before rewriting a prompt out of frustration, ask explicitly: *"was that a prompt problem, a context problem, or a harness problem?"* This is a diagnostic framework, not a technique in itself — the real risk is defaulting to "just reword it" out of habit even when the actual gap sits one or two layers further out.

#### 2.2 Talking to AI Studio well: a checklist, and the habit most people skip

**The checklist: Role, Task, Format, Constraints.** This exact four-part shape (sometimes called RTF, sometimes bundled into longer acronyms like CO-STAR or CARE) isn't a single canonical framework from one source — multiple independent prompting guides converge on the same core ingredients because they map onto the actual channels a model reads (see the reference course's own "anatomy of a prompt" for the fuller channel breakdown). The practical version for AI Studio:

- **Role/perspective** — who should the AI "be" for this answer? ("As a risk analyst...")
- **Task** — the specific, concrete ask, stated with a specific verb. "Look at" and "help with" force the model to guess what you actually want; "summarize," "compare," "flag," "extract" don't.
- **Format** — how the answer should be structured: a bullet list, a table, a specific chart type, a number with explicit units.
- **Constraints** — what to exclude, which sources to use or not use, tone, length, time window.

**The habit most people skip: explain *why*, not just *what*.** This is genuinely underused, and it's disproportionately effective. Giving the model the *reason* behind an instruction — not only the instruction — lets it correctly generalize to cases you didn't explicitly anticipate. Anthropic's own framing is a useful way to internalize this: treat the model like a brilliant but brand-new colleague with zero context on your norms, and apply the golden rule — if a colleague with minimal context would be confused by an instruction, so will the model ([Anthropic, "Claude prompting best practices"](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). A constraint stated flatly ("never include client names") only covers the literal case you thought to write down; a constraint stated with its reason ("never include client names — this output may be pasted into a shared team channel") correctly generalizes to other things that would violate the same underlying concern, even ones you didn't list.

**Before/after.** `[DRAFT — placeholder for structure, not a finalized example]`
- Vague: *"Show me the desk's exposure."*
- Effective: *"As a risk analyst, summarize our FX desk's notional exposure by currency pair as a bar chart, using today's end-of-day positions only, excluding hedged positions, because this is going straight into the morning risk pack and needs to reflect only live directional risk."*

Note what the *reason clause* at the end buys you beyond the bare constraint: if a new, related edge case comes up later that wasn't explicitly listed (say, a partially-hedged position), the model has a principle to reason from ("this is about live directional risk") rather than only a literal rule to pattern-match against.

**Why this matters.** AI Studio's agent-selection and dashboard-generation behavior respond measurably better to specificity — a vague prompt forces the underlying agent to guess which tool, format, and scope you actually meant, which is exactly where mismatches originate. The wording overhead is a few extra seconds; the reliability gain compounds every time the same prompt pattern gets reused.

#### 2.3 Conversations, not one-shots: managing multi-turn context deliberately

**The mental model.** AI Studio conversations are not single-shot: a follow-up carries forward everything said before, up to the model's context-window limit, rather than starting from a blank slate. Each turn appends to what the model sees, which is exactly why a follow-up like *"now break that down by trader"* works without re-specifying the whole original question — and exactly why an early wrong assumption, left uncorrected, can quietly pollute every answer that follows it.

**What actually degrades in a long conversation.** This isn't a vague caution — it's an active, measured phenomenon. Long-running sessions accumulate contradictions, drift from the user's original intent, and — in some documented cases — repetitive "sycophancy loops," where the model increasingly just agrees with whatever was said most recently rather than reasoning fresh ([Tian Pan, "Long-Session Context Degradation"](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-19-long-session-context-degradation-multi-turn)). Importantly, a large advertised context window is not a *reliability* guarantee — a model that technically accepts 100K+ tokens says nothing about whether it reasons *coherently* across all of them once you're deep into a long back-and-forth.

This is a genuinely useful correction to the instinct to treat "how many turns before I should restart" as a fixed number. There isn't a universal magic turn-count — the better signal is behavioral: if the model starts repeating itself, contradicting something it said three turns ago, or visibly losing precision on the original ask, that's the moment to restart, not a specific number of exchanges.

**When to follow up vs. start fresh.** Follow up when you're refining or drilling into the same underlying question — that's what multi-turn is *for*. Start fresh when you're switching topics entirely, or when you notice the conversation showing the degradation signals above (repetition, contradiction, drifting off the original ask).

**How to apply.** Treat your first prompt as a rough draft, not a final answer — plan on two or three refining turns as the normal path to a good result, not a sign that your first prompt failed. And don't be precious about restarting: a fresh conversation is a legitimate reset, not a failure to use the tool properly.

#### 2.4 From message to fields: designing prompts for structured output

**The mental model.** Whenever an AI-generated answer needs to feed something downstream — a script, a form, a comparison across many similar inputs — free-form prose is the wrong target, no matter how well-written it is. The fix is to design the prompt to explicitly request a fixed schema: named fields, in a stated format, rather than a paragraph the reader (or another system) has to parse by hand.

**How it works, concretely.** State the schema explicitly — *"extract: trader name, instrument, notional, direction — as a table with exactly these four columns"* — and give one worked example of the desired shape if the schema is at all non-trivial. Crucially, also state what should happen for a field that doesn't apply: instruct the model to return `null` or "not mentioned" rather than letting it guess a plausible-sounding value to fill the gap — ambiguity about missing data is a common, avoidable source of silently wrong downstream results.

**Where this sits relative to the platform layer.** Asking nicely for a format in a prompt is *usually* followed reliably by current models, but it is meaningfully weaker than an API-level feature that actually constrains what tokens the model is allowed to produce. Both OpenAI and Anthropic now offer schema-constrained generation modes at the API level specifically because "please respond in JSON" is a strong convention, not a hard guarantee — OpenAI's own benchmark found their newer schema-constrained mode scoring effectively 100% on complex schema-following tasks, versus under 40% for an older model relying on prompted formatting alone ([OpenAI, "Introducing Structured Outputs in the API"](https://openai.com/index/introducing-structured-outputs-in-the-api/)). For this audience, the practical implication is: within AI Studio, prompting explicitly for a schema (as above) is the right and only lever available at the consumer level — but *if* AI Studio exposes a genuine schema-constrained output mode under the hood for agent-builders, that would be a strictly more reliable option than prompted formatting for anything feeding a real downstream system. `[internal — verify with your platform team whether AI Studio/AI Lens expose true schema-constrained output for agent configuration, or only prompted formatting]`

**When to use structured output.** Any time the output feeds another step (a script, a database, a form), or needs aggregating/comparing across many instances — e.g., extracting the same four fields from fifty separate trader messages so they can be tabulated.

**Why it matters here specifically.** This is the exact mechanism behind "parse a trader's message into structured fields" — one of this audience's actual daily AI Studio use cases. Without explicit structure instructions, the model defaults to prose, which is hard for a downstream system (and, often, even a person skimming quickly) to reliably use.

#### 2.5 AI as translator: bridging business and quant phrasing

**The mental model.** In a genuinely unified room — business people and quant developers together, with no separate technical track — one recurring, real cost is translation friction: a business ask loses precision on its way to becoming a technical specification, and a technical result loses its caveats on the way back up to a business audience. A language model is well-suited to sitting in that gap, because reformulating one register of language into another is fundamentally a language task, which is exactly what these models are built for.

**How to apply it.** Prompt explicitly for the *direction* of translation rather than leaving it implicit:
- Business → technical: *"formalize this into a precise technical requirement a quant developer could implement directly."* (e.g., turning "I want to know if we're too exposed to rate moves" into "compute DV01 across the book, bucketed by tenor.")
- Technical → business: *"explain this the way a trading desk head would want it explained, with no jargon, and flag any caveat a non-technical reader needs to know before acting on it."*

**Why it matters.** This shortcuts a real, recurring cost of cross-functional work without requiring either side to become fluent in the other's vocabulary from scratch.

**Pros/cons, and an honest caveat.** This is a genuinely useful drafting aid for scoping a new request or explaining a result — but it should not replace direct conversation for anything ambiguous or high-stakes. A caveat worth stating plainly here since it doesn't get said enough elsewhere in the series: this specific application — "AI as a business/quant translator" — is applied reasoning from the model's known general strengths in reformulation and summarization, not a pattern independently validated in the external prompting or context-engineering literature searched for this document. ### Session 3: Beyond the Prompt: RAG, Steering, and Fine-Tuning Explained

#### 3.1 The control ladder: six rungs, and which ones are actually yours

**The mental model.** There is a real hierarchy of ways to change what a large language model does, running from "affects this one conversation" to "affects every model of this kind, forever, at a cost only a handful of organizations on Earth can pay." Every rung above the last one buys more control, at a steep and non-linear increase in cost, complexity, and how long the change takes to make. Two genuinely distinct mechanisms sit underneath all six rungs: **prompting** shapes behavior indirectly, by influencing which parts of the model's existing knowledge get activated for a given input, without touching the model's weights at all; **fine-tuning and pre-training** shape behavior by directly changing those weights. This distinction matters because it explains *why* the top of the ladder is so much more expensive than the bottom — you're not just writing different words, you're retraining a machine.

**Rung 1 — Few-shot prompting.** Giving the model a handful of example input→output pairs directly inside a single prompt, showing it the pattern you want. Lowest control, lowest cost — nothing beyond writing a few good examples — but the effect is scoped to that one conversation and has to be repeated every time, and is limited by how much context window you can spend on examples versus everything else you need in view.

**Rung 2 — System prompt.** A standing instruction set applied to *every* interaction with a given agent, typically invisible to the end user — it sets persona, defaults, and guardrails once, at configuration time, rather than per query. This is a materially higher rung than few-shot because it persists across an entire agent's lifetime rather than one conversation — but it's set once by whoever configures the agent (in this audience's split, usually a quant developer building in AI Lens), not adjusted per-query by the person asking a question. **For most people in this room, the system prompt rung is roughly the ceiling of what they can influence directly** — worth stating to the room plainly, because it recalibrates expectations about how much control a business-side user actually has day to day.

**Rung 3 — LLM steering.** This is worth being precise about, because the term gets used loosely and it's easy to accidentally teach something narrower or vaguer than what it actually names. The plain-language version first: **a system prompt tells the model, in words, what to do; fine-tuning permanently edits the model's memory; steering sits in between — it nudges the model's internal "state of mind" mid-response, without changing its words-in instructions and without retraining anything.** For anyone who wants the more precise mechanism behind that: in the current technical literature, steering specifically means intervening directly on the model's internal activations at inference time — nudging its internal representations toward a desired behavior, computed by comparing how the model's internals differ between a "do this" example and a "don't do this" example — without retraining any weights ([arXiv, "Steer Like the LLM: Activation Steering that Mimics Prompting"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.03907)). Steering sits genuinely between prompting and fine-tuning in both mechanism and power — but it is a research-and-platform-level technique, typically something a model provider or platform builds *into* a product, not something an end user (or, usually, even an agent-builder in a UI) configures directly by name. `[internal — verify with your platform team whether anything AI Lens exposes as "tone" or "response style" configuration is genuine activation-level steering, or whether it is actually a more sophisticated system-prompt/parameter layer wearing a steering-sounding label — the distinction matters if a technically literate quant developer in the room asks how it actually works under the hood]`

**Rung 4 — Fine-tuning.** Adjusting the model's weights on a custom dataset so its default behavior changes permanently for that specific fine-tuned copy — more powerful and more consistent than any amount of prompting, because the change is baked into the model rather than re-supplied every time. It needs labeled training data, real infrastructure, and ongoing maintenance as the underlying base model evolves. **This is not available anywhere in this audience's current stack — taught here conceptually only**, so that the room understands what it would unlock, not as a call to action.

**Rung 5 — Instruction fine-tuning.** Worth a precise correction to a common mental shortcut: instruction fine-tuning isn't "more" fine-tuning stacked above rung 4 — it's a specific *type* of fine-tuning, and historically an earlier one in a model's lifecycle. Instruction tuning is what teaches a raw, pre-trained model to interpret an input as an instruction requiring a helpful response, rather than as text to simply continue statistically — it is, mechanically, the step that turns a base language model into something that behaves like a "chat" model at all ([IBM, "What Is Instruction Tuning?"](https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/instruction-tuning)). It typically sits *before* the kind of domain-specific fine-tuning an individual enterprise might later apply, and is often followed by a further alignment step — reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) — that trains the model to prefer responses matching human raters' judgments of quality and safety ([RLHF Book, "Instruction Fine-Tuning"](https://rlhfbook.com/c/04-instruction-tuning)). Mention this rung as the underlying mechanism that already happened, upstream, to make the chat model this audience uses possible at all — not something this audience would ever do themselves.

**Rung 6 — Pre-training.** Training a model from scratch on a massive text corpus — the foundation everything else sits on top of. The most powerful, most expensive rung by an enormous margin (compute cost at a scale only a small number of frontier labs operate at), and the least practically relevant lever anyone in this room would ever pull. One line on the slide, no more — mentioned purely to complete the ladder conceptually.

**The decision guide that actually matters day to day.**
- *"Do I just need this one response to look different?"* → few-shot (rung 1).
- *"Do I need every interaction with this agent to behave a certain way?"* → system prompt (rung 2) — and in practice, that means a conversation with whoever built the agent, not something to configure yourself.
- *"Do I need the model's fundamental knowledge or behavior to change permanently?"* → this is where fine-tuning (rung 4) sounds like the answer, but in practice — see §3.2 — retrieval usually solves the actual underlying need without touching a single weight.

**Why this matters.** This gives a genuine mental model for "how much can I actually change this thing's behavior, and at what real cost" — most of this audience's day-to-day influence sits at rungs 1–2, rung 3 is, at most, partially reachable indirectly via whatever configuration AI Lens exposes, and rungs 4–6 are conceptual-only for now, by explicit design of this session.

#### 3.2 The real fork: RAG vs. fine-tuning

**The mental model.** Once "fine-tuning isn't available to us today" has been said plainly, the practical question this audience actually faces is not "prompt vs. fine-tune" — it's **RAG vs. fine-tuning**, because RAG is the practically reachable alternative for a need that might otherwise feel like it demands fine-tuning. The cleanest one-line distinction: RAG retrieves relevant information and lets the model *read* it at the moment it answers — giving the model new knowledge, temporarily, for that one query; fine-tuning trains the model on labeled examples in advance — giving the model new default behavior, permanently, for every future query ([Winder.AI, "RAG vs Fine-Tuning: A Decision Framework"](https://winder.ai/rag-vs-fine-tuning-2026-decision-framework/)). Or, stated as the sharpest possible summary: **fine-tuning is for form, not facts** — it shapes tone, style, structured-output habits, and refusal patterns, not knowledge that changes week to week.

**How RAG works, mechanically, step by step:**
1. A knowledge base — documents, structured data, or both — is broken into chunks and indexed, typically via embeddings and vector search.
2. A query comes in; the system retrieves the chunks most relevant to it.
3. Those retrieved chunks are inserted into the model's context, alongside the question.
4. The model answers grounded in that retrieved context, rather than relying purely on what it happened to learn during training.

This is exactly the mechanism AI Lens implements when a team "attaches a knowledge base" to an agent — the platform is doing steps 1–3 automatically so the agent-builder only has to supply the documents and configure the retrieval, not build a search index by hand.

**Why RAG is the correct default for most enterprise needs in 2026, not just this audience's fallback.** Current industry consensus treats RAG as the right default choice specifically because organizational knowledge needs to stay current — customer support, internal knowledge management, compliance Q&A, and any application where answers must be accurate, current, and traceable back to a source all favor retrieval over baking facts into weights that go stale the moment the underlying policy or market data changes. Academic comparison work reaches the same conclusion from a different angle: a controlled study comparing knowledge injection via fine-tuning against retrieval-based approaches found retrieval-augmented methods generally outperformed fine-tuning for injecting new factual knowledge, particularly for less-common information the base model hadn't seen much of during pre-training ([Ovadia et al., "Fine-Tuning or Retrieval? Comparing Knowledge Injection in LLMs"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.05934)).

**The honest complication worth naming: most sophisticated production systems now use both.** This isn't an either/or in the most advanced deployments — current industry reporting describes roughly 60% of production GenAI projects in 2025–2026 combining fine-tuning and RAG, using fine-tuning to control *how* the model responds (style, format, reasoning approach) while RAG controls *what* it responds with (current, accurate, sourceable facts). This is worth stating honestly to a technically literate room even though fine-tuning isn't in this audience's stack today — it's the accurate long-run picture, not a reason to feel behind: the hybrid pattern is exactly the RAG half of what this audience *can* already do in AI Lens, plus a fine-tuning half that simply isn't reachable yet.

**The decision guide:**
- *"Is this about facts or knowledge that changes?"* → RAG.
- *"Is this about consistent tone, style, or behavior that should apply automatically, every time, without re-explaining it?"* → steering or a system prompt today; fine-tuning would eventually be the fuller answer, once available.
- *"Is this a one-off need?"* → don't build anything — just prompt or use a few-shot example (rung 1).

**What's actually involved in a RAG setup, concretely, in AI Lens terms.** A knowledge base (the source documents or data); an indexing/embedding step (handled by the platform, invisible to the agent-builder); retrieval configuration (how many chunks come back per query, what similarity threshold counts as "relevant enough"); and the agent itself, which consumes whatever gets retrieved as part of its context for that query.

**Pros/cons.** RAG is transparent (you can trace which source passage informed an answer) and fast to update (edit the knowledge base, no retraining) — but retrieval can miss the genuinely relevant chunk, or return noisy near-misses, if the knowledge base is poorly organized or the retrieval settings are badly tuned. Fine-tuning produces deeply consistent, "baked-in" behavior that doesn't need to be re-stated every time — but it's expensive, slow to update when anything changes, and, for this audience today, simply not available.

### Session 4: Guardrails, Verification, and Compliance: The Trust Toolkit

#### 4.1 Guardrails: what to recognize and ask for, not what to build

**The mental model.** Guardrails are mechanisms that constrain what an AI system is allowed to do or say, and — critically for this audience — they typically sit *around* the model, not inside it, which is exactly why a non-builder can meaningfully evaluate whether they exist without needing to understand how the underlying model works. Current practitioner guidance describes a layered architecture that's worth teaching as a mental map even to people who will never build one: **input rails** filter what a user is even allowed to ask before it reaches the model; **dialog rails** shape the conversational flow and what the model is allowed to reason about mid-conversation; **output rails** filter or modify what the model produces before a person ever sees it ([Humanloop, "LLM Guardrails"](https://humanloop.com/blog/llm-guardrails)). Layered on top of all three: **permissioning** — what data and tools an agent is even allowed to touch — and **human-in-the-loop checkpoints**, which require explicit approval before some action actually executes.

**Why input filtering alone is not enough — a nuance worth including.** Current enterprise guidance is explicit that filtering only what goes *in* is insufficient, because a model's output can still violate ethical, legal, or organizational policy even from a perfectly reasonable input — which is exactly why output-side checks (bias/harmful-content detection, policy-compliance checks for things like financial-disclosure rules) exist as a separate, necessary layer rather than a redundant one ([Humanloop, "LLM Guardrails"](https://humanloop.com/blog/llm-guardrails)).

**Why this matters here specifically.** Guardrails are what make it *safe* to give an agent real capabilities — querying sensitive data, drafting something client-facing, taking an action — without them, an agent's flexibility becomes a liability rather than an asset. This directly motivates the framing of this entire topic for a non-builder audience: **you don't need to build these — you need the habit of asking whether they exist** before trusting an agent with anything sensitive, exactly the same diligence you'd apply to any other new system touching sensitive data or client information.

**When to ask about which kind.** Workflow touches sensitive data → ask about input/output filtering. Workflow can take a real-world action (sending something, updating a system) → ask about human-in-the-loop approval, specifically what's reversible enough to proceed without it and what isn't. Genuinely unsure what an agent can access → ask directly about its permissioning before using it for anything beyond casual exploration.

#### 4.2 AI safety and trust as a consumer: calibrating reliance

**The mental model, reframed deliberately for this room.** Most of this audience is not building AI systems; they're deciding whether to rely on one someone else built. The right question isn't "is this system safe in the abstract" — it's "have I calibrated my reliance on it correctly for this specific use." Research on human-AI decision-making gives this a precise name: **appropriate reliance** is a user's ability to accept correct advice from a system *and* identify and reject incorrect advice — not simply "trust it" or "distrust it" as a blanket stance ([arXiv, "From Trust to Appropriate Reliance"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.23896)).

**A genuinely counter-intuitive finding worth including, because it corrects a natural instinct.** It would be reasonable to assume that a system explaining its reasoning is automatically safer to rely on than one that doesn't. Current research says otherwise, and the finding is specific and well-documented: providing an explanation for an AI's output can *increase* overreliance rather than reduce it, because people tend to read the mere presence of a detailed-sounding explanation as a general signal of competence, rather than actually evaluating that specific explanation's content — and more detailed explanations produce *more* trust and *more* overreliance, not more appropriately-calibrated trust ([Microsoft Research (Aether), "Overreliance on AI: A Literature Review"](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/Aether-Overreliance-on-AI-Review-Final-6.21.22.pdf)). This is an important thing to say out loud to a room of smart, technically literate people, because "the AI explained its reasoning, so I trust the number" is precisely the failure mode this finding describes.

**The practical questions of a careful reviewer, asked as a consumer rather than a builder:** *"has this been tested on cases like mine, specifically?" "what happens, concretely, if it's wrong?" "is there a way to report a bad output, and does anyone act on those reports?" "who is actually accountable if this causes a real problem?"*

**Why this matters, stated plainly for a regulated environment.** "The AI said so" is never, on its own, an acceptable justification in a regulated financial environment — the same diligence already applied to any other new tool or process applies here without exception, and arguably applies *more* given the counter-intuitive explanation-overreliance finding above.

**When to raise this.** Before adopting a new agent or tool for anything beyond casual, low-stakes exploration — and especially before it touches anything client-facing, compliance-sensitive, or decision-influencing.

#### 4.3 Verify before you trust: hallucination-awareness for financial numbers

**The mental model, and the more precise term worth knowing.** "Hallucination" is the term that stuck in popular usage, but it's worth knowing the more precise alternative that current standards bodies increasingly use alongside it: **confabulation**. The distinction is more than pedantic — a hallucination, in the original clinical sense, is a false *perception*; a confabulation is a mistaken *reconstruction*, influenced by existing patterns, expectations, and context, without any perception involved at all. Since a language model has no sensory experience to hallucinate from in the first place, "confabulation" is arguably the mechanically more accurate term for what's actually happening: the model isn't "seeing" something that isn't there — it's generating a plausible-sounding continuation that happens to be wrong ([PLOS Digital Health, "Hallucination or Confabulation? Neuroanatomy as metaphor in Large Language Models"](https://journals.plos.org/digitalhealth/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pdig.0000388)). NIST's own generative-AI risk guidance uses both terms together for exactly this reason — "confabulation or hallucination" — naming the risk of models generating factually inconsistent or fabricated content as one of the generative-AI-specific risks organizations need to manage ([NIST AI 600-1, Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework)).

**Why this risk compounds specifically around numbers, and specifically around calculations.** Current benchmark research on financial-domain tasks found a striking and specific pattern worth knowing precisely rather than vaguely: models that perform well on simple factual lookups can degrade dramatically on tasks requiring multi-step numerical reasoning — one benchmark found leading models collapsing from roughly 95% accuracy on simple lookups to near-zero accuracy on multivariate calculations ([JurisTech, "2026 LLM Benchmark For AI Hallucination in Finance"](https://juristech.net/juristechs-2026-llm-benchmark-for-ai-hallucination-in-finance/)). This is a meaningfully sharper and more specific finding than a generic "AI can get numbers wrong" caution: the risk isn't uniform across all numerical tasks — a model correctly retrieving a single stated figure is a fundamentally different reliability profile than the same model computing something that requires combining several figures across several steps. The practical upshot for this audience: **the more steps of calculation stand between the source data and the number in front of you, the more verification it deserves** — a rule proportional to actual measured risk, not a blanket "always distrust every number."

**The habit, concretely, in order:**
1. **Cross-check the source** — does the number or claim trace to something you can actually verify, independently of the AI's say-so?
2. **Sanity-check the magnitude** — does it feel plausible given what you already know: order of magnitude, direction, recent history?
3. **For anything material, spot-check one data point by hand** — especially anything that involved a multi-step calculation rather than a direct lookup, per the finding above.

**When to verify more vs. less rigorously.** Scale scrutiny to both stakes and structure: the higher the materiality (client-facing, decision-driving) *and* the more calculation-heavy the claim, the more verification is warranted. A quick synthesis of well-known, easily-checked information warrants less scrutiny than a specific number that fed several steps of derivation and now feeds a real decision.

#### 4.4 The troubleshooting checklist: prompt, context, tool, or data

**The mental model.** This extends Session 2's three-layer prompt/context/harness framework with a fourth layer that sits entirely outside the AI system itself, and walking all four in order converts "the AI is broken" — a dead end — into "which specific layer actually failed" — an actionable diagnosis.

1. **Prompt** — was the ask clear, specific, and unambiguous?
2. **Context** — did the model actually have the information it needed: the right documents attached, the right facts present in view?
3. **Tool/agent choice** — was the right agent or tool even selected for this task (the harness layer, from Session 2)?
4. **Data** — is the underlying data itself wrong, stale, or missing? This is not an AI failure at all — it's a data-quality problem that happens to be surfacing through an AI-generated output, and no amount of prompt or context tuning fixes it.

**How to apply.** Walk the list roughly in this order — it's generally faster to rule out cheap, simple explanations (wording) before assuming something structural (the underlying data itself) is wrong, though an experienced user should feel free to jump straight to whichever layer past experience suggests is the likely culprit for a given kind of failure.

**Why this matters.** This is arguably the single most reusable skill in the entire series for people who already use these tools daily — it turns a recurring moment of frustration into a fast, systematic diagnosis, and it's the direct payoff of having taught the three-layer framework back in Session 2.

#### 4.5 MNPI and compliance-aware AI use in Global Markets `[compliance-sensitive — flagged throughout]`

**Why this is not a new risk, just a faster and less visible version of an old one.** Material Non-Public Information (MNPI) — confidential information about a company or security that hasn't been publicly released and would likely influence an investment decision — sits at the center of insider-trading rules, and has always required discretion in email, chat, and shared drives ([Smarsh, "What is Material Non-Public Information (MNPI)?"](https://www.smarsh.com/compliance-glossary/material-non-public-information/)). An AI system with broad context or knowledge-base access introduces a new *mechanism* for the same old risk to occur — inadvertently surfacing information across an information barrier it should never cross, e.g., a knowledge base that mixes content from two desks meant to be walled off from each other — but it is not a fundamentally new category of risk. What's genuinely new is speed and visibility: the mixing can happen instantly and invisibly, inside a single AI-generated answer, rather than through a slower, more traceable human channel.

**The regulatory reality worth citing plainly: existing rules already apply, and regulators have said so explicitly.** It's worth being precise here rather than treating this as a hypothetical future risk: US financial regulators have already confirmed that existing, technology-neutral rules cover generative AI the same way they cover any other channel. FINRA's Regulatory Notice 24-09 explicitly confirmed that existing rules reach generative AI use ([Mondaq, "When AI Models Access Nonpublic Information: MNPI Risks For Financial Firms"](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/new-technology/1820450/when-ai-models-access-nonpublic-information-mnpi-risks-for-financial-firms)). The specific rules that already govern this, regardless of whether AI is involved: Rule 10b-5 and the misappropriation theory (no trading on, or misusing, MNPI); Regulation FD (no selective disclosure of MNPI); and FINRA Rule 3110 (a firm's obligation to supervise for MNPI misuse and maintain information barriers). The core mechanical risk to control, stated as simply as possible: a single prompt can carry MNPI into a system that stores it, logs it, or — in a fine-tuning scenario, which is exactly why this matters even conceptually — trains on it.

**The scale of the underlying gap this sits inside.** It's worth grounding this in real numbers rather than treating it as an abstract concern: the Bank of England and FCA's most recent joint survey of AI and machine learning across UK financial services found that among the top perceived risks, four of the top five are data-related — data privacy, data quality, data security, and data bias — and, more pointedly, only 34% of firms report "complete understanding" of the AI systems they actually use, with 46% reporting only partial understanding ([FCA, "Research Note: AI in UK financial services"](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/research-notes/ai-uk-financial-services)). That gap between deployment and understanding is exactly the environment in which an MNPI-crossing mistake becomes more, not less, likely.

**What must never go into a prompt, context window, or knowledge base** `[NEEDS COMPLIANCE SIGN-OFF — the following is illustrative structure only, not settled policy, and must not be presented to an audience without real review]`: client-identifying information beyond what a task genuinely requires; MNPI relevant to a pending deal or transaction; anything that would cross a defined information barrier between desks; anything that would violate a specific client confidentiality agreement.

**The fast gut-check, for a person, not a system, to apply.** *"Would I be comfortable saying this out loud in a room that includes people from every desk?"* If not, it doesn't go into a prompt, a conversation, or a knowledge base either — the same discretion that has always applied to email and chat applies here, unchanged in substance, just newly relevant to a new channel.

**Why this differs from §4.1's guardrails.** Platform-level guardrails are about what the *system* technically prevents. This is about individual judgment and discipline that no platform control fully substitutes for — the responsibility for what goes into a prompt sits with the person typing it, not only with whatever filtering the platform happens to have configured.

What fresh research sharpened was the *evidence behind the warning*: existing material said hallucination risk "compounds" for financial numbers in general terms; current benchmark research gives a specific, striking, and more actionable finding — accuracy can collapse from ~95% on simple lookups to near-zero on multivariate calculations. I used this to sharpen the guidance from "verify numbers more" to "verify numbers more, and specifically verify anything that involved a multi-step calculation more than anything that was a direct lookup" — a more precise, defensible, and genuinely more useful rule. ### Session 5: Bringing It Home: AI in Practice, Start to Finish

#### 5.1 AI with Finance: where it plausibly fits across a Global Markets workflow

**The mental model.** This session's job is to show the whole toolbox — prompt, context, harness, verification — working together on one realistic task, live and narrated, rather than leaving five sessions' worth of concepts sitting disconnected in the audience's notes. Before the demo itself, it's worth mapping, at a glance, where AI plausibly fits across a typical desk workflow — deliberately framed as "plausibly fits," not "already does all of this well," since overclaiming here would directly undercut Session 4's entire trust framework:

- **Pre-trade research and analysis** — summarizing market commentary, surfacing relevant precedent.
- **Trade capture/structuring assistance** — turning a natural-language description into structured fields (this is Session 2's structured-output topic, applied).
- **Post-trade reporting and summarization** — turning raw data into a readable summary (Session 1's dashboard-generation pattern, applied).
- **Client communication drafting** — a starting draft only, always human-reviewed, given Session 4's verification and MNPI framing.
- **Risk/exposure monitoring** — surfacing patterns worth a human's attention, not replacing the human's judgment about what to do with them.

**Grounding this in real adoption data, not aspiration.** It's worth telling this room honestly where the industry actually is, not where the hype cycle claims it is: the Bank of England and FCA's most recent joint survey found three-quarters of UK financial firms already deploying AI in some form, but with foundation models/LLMs specifically accounting for only 17% of total AI use cases, and — tellingly — only 2% of AI use cases across the industry operating fully autonomously, with the large majority retaining meaningful human oversight ([FCA, "Research Note: AI in UK financial services"](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/research-notes/ai-uk-financial-services)). This is a genuinely useful number to open the session with: it tells the room they are not behind an industry that has already fully automated these workflows — they're at roughly the same point most of the industry actually is, human oversight and all.

**Delivery note.** Live-coded or notebook demo, fully narrated — no code-along, per the format's standing decision. Sai writes and runs code, or runs a pre-built notebook while explaining each step; nobody in the room is expected to code alongside.

#### 5.2 Faster legacy and undocumented code understanding using AI

**The mental model.** Using AI to read and explain undocumented or sparsely-documented existing code is a direct answer to a very common, very real pain point in quant/trading codebases: the original author has moved on, comments are sparse or absent, and understanding what a piece of legacy code actually does — before anyone dares change it — becomes a slow, manual "archaeology" exercise.

**How it works, and why the model is well-suited to it.** Feed the model the code in question and ask it to explain what it does, in what order, what edge cases it appears to handle, and what looks unusual or risky. Code has strong local syntactic and structural regularity that a model can parse reliably even without full business context — though supplying that business context, via a prompt or via RAG against internal documentation, measurably improves the accuracy of the explanation.

**The honest, evidence-based caveat worth including — sharper than a blanket productivity claim.** It would be easy to state this topic as an unqualified win, and existing writing on this subject sometimes does. Current systematic research is more careful, and worth representing accurately to a room that includes the actual developers who would be judging this claim against their own experience: the literature does report considerable, real benefits from LLM-assisted code understanding — accelerated development, reduced code-search time, automation of repetitive explanation work — but multiple systematic reviews are explicit that these benefits are **not evenly distributed across all users**, that much of the supporting research is still exploratory, and that there are real, documented concerns about cognitive offloading and reduced team collaboration that come with leaning on an AI explanation instead of building understanding directly ([arXiv, "The Impact of LLM-Assistants on Software Developer Productivity: A Systematic Literature Review"](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.03156v1)). The honest framing for this room: expect a real, first-pass orientation benefit — not a uniform, guaranteed win for every person or every codebase, and not a substitute for actually testing behavior before trusting an AI-generated explanation enough to act on it.

**When to use it, and when not to.** Excellent as a first-pass explanation or orientation tool for something you're about to touch. Not a substitute for actually testing the code's real behavior before relying on the AI's explanation to make a change — this is the same "trust but verify" theme from Session 4's §4.3, applied to code instead of financial numbers.

**How to apply.** Paste the function or module and ask: *"explain this step by step, flag anything that looks unusual or risky, and tell me specifically what I should test before changing it."*

#### 5.3 Picking the right agents and tools: a selection heuristic

**The mental model.** This extends Session 1's "what is an agent" concept into an actual, repeatable decision process for a real AI Studio workflow: given a task, which agent(s) or tool(s) should actually be selected before asking the question?

**The heuristic, as three questions:**
1. **What's the task** — a single, well-defined action, or does it genuinely span multiple domains?
2. **What's the input** — a question, a document, live data?
3. **What output do I actually need** — a number, a dashboard, structured data, prose?

Match these to the *smallest* set of agents/tools that plausibly covers the need, and add more only if the first, narrower attempt genuinely falls short — resist the instinct to select everything available "just in case."

**Why this matters, and an honest note on how novel this actually is.** It's worth being candid that this exact heuristic isn't lifted from an established external framework, because — as current research on enterprise agent selection makes clear — no single, well-validated framework for this problem yet exists industry-wide: current academic and practitioner writing explicitly describes agent-selection decisions today as largely "intuition-driven rather than evidence-based," precisely because a principled, general framework for when and how to select among multiple agents has been missing from the field so far ([arXiv, "A cybersecurity AI agent selection and decision support framework"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.01751); see also the emerging "STRIDE" framework for selecting among agentic vs. assistant vs. single-call AI modalities, [arXiv, 2512.02228](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.02228), as an early sign the field is starting to formalize this). That's not a weakness of teaching this heuristic — it's actually a reason it's worth teaching plainly and confidently: this room is being given a genuinely useful, reasoned starting heuristic for a problem the wider industry hasn't yet standardized either, not a shortcut version of some established best practice they're missing.

**Why over-selecting is a real cost, not just an aesthetic preference.** Selecting more agents/tools than a task needs can slow down and confuse the response, because the underlying system now has more options to reason across before committing to a path — this is a genuinely underused piece of judgment, since most people either always default to the same single familiar tool, or select everything available out of an understandable but counterproductive instinct to be thorough.

#### 5.4 Making it stick: building a team prompt/agent-library habit

**The mental model.** A five-session series has real, immediate value in the room — and then decays fast the moment everyone disperses back to their desks, unless something concrete captures it. A lightweight, shared prompt-and-agent-configuration library is the mechanism that prevents that decay: a low-friction, shared location where someone who found a prompt or agent setup that worked well posts it, with enough context that a colleague can reuse it directly rather than rediscovering the same technique independently, weeks later, from scratch.

**Why this is a validated, real practice, not a nice-sounding idea invented for this series.** Enterprise prompt libraries are an increasingly standard, well-documented practice, not a novel suggestion: a structured, shared collection of reusable AI instructions — effectively an organizational "AI playbook" — with each entry carrying enough structured metadata (what it's for, expected inputs, expected output format, an owner) that someone else can pick it up and use it correctly the first time ([C# Corner, "Enterprise Prompt Libraries: Managing Reusable AI Instructions at Scale"](https://www.c-sharpcorner.com/article/enterprise-prompt-libraries-managing-reusable-ai-instructions-at-scale/)). Note on a specific number worth treating skeptically: some vendor-published material claims very large, specific ROI multipliers for having a mature prompt library versus an informal one — those numbers come from vendor research rather than independent, peer-reviewed study, and are not repeated here as fact; the *qualitative* claim (a curated, maintained library outperforms an ad hoc one, and it takes real, sustained effort to build rather than happening automatically) is well-supported and worth keeping regardless of the specific multiplier.

**How to apply, concretely.** Assign informal ownership — someone who curates and prunes the library so it doesn't silently rot into an unmaintained pile of stale prompts — and seed it *live*, in this session, with today's demo prompt as its first real entry, so the library exists as a going concern before the room disperses, rather than as an action item that quietly never happens.

**Why this matters as the series' actual closing note.** This is what converts five one-hour sessions into a compounding team asset instead of a fading memory of a good talk — the value taught across this entire series decays specifically because it isn't captured and reused by default, and this habit is the direct, concrete mechanism that interrupts that decay.
